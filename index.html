Market Sizing — What I'm Learning

Market sizing is a skill I've been actively building. Here's how I've been practising it — using the Packaged Drinking Water market as a simple example to understand the core approach.

What is Market Sizing?

At its core, market sizing is answering: "How big is this market?" — not with a guess, but with a structured, logical estimate built from real data.

In my work in market research, I've seen market sizing referenced constantly — in client reports, feasibility studies, and competitive analysis. I decided to actually learn the basic methods so I can contribute more meaningfully to these deliverables.

I'm still in the early stages — but the approach I've practised is: start with a real market, use free official data, apply a simple method, and sanity-check the result.

Practice Market

I chose Global Packaged Drinking Water because it's a straightforward consumer market — easy to understand, with clear data available from WHO, FAO, and World Bank.

Two Methods I Practised
Top-Down — Start from the big picture

Take a large known number (world GDP or total beverage market) and narrow it down using percentage estimates at each level until you reach your product.

World GDP → F&B Sector (4.2%)
→ Beverages → Packaged Water
≈ $315 Bn estimate
Bottom-Up — Build from individual units

Estimate who buys the product, how much they buy, and at what price — then multiply up to get the total market size. More granular and defensible.

Population × % without tap water (WHO)
× Litres/year × Price per litre
≈ $256–326 Bn range

What I Learned

The core formula is simple

Forecast Year = Base Year × (1 + Growth Rate)^n. Once I understood this, building the table in Excel was straightforward — I just needed reliable numbers to plug in.

Data sourcing is half the work

I used WHO drinking water reports, World Bank GDP data, and UN Comtrade for trade volumes. Finding the right free data source was more challenging than the calculation itself.

Cross-checking gives confidence

Running both top-down and bottom-up methods and comparing results (I got ~$315Bn vs ~$320Bn) showed me that the estimate was directionally right — even if not perfectly precise.

What I want to improve

I'm working on getting better at setting growth rate assumptions with more rigour, and understanding regional splits. This is an area I'm actively building on.
